# Newton & Leibniz — A General Calculus and the Fundamental Theorem (1666–1686)

#person #milestone #read #calculus #fundamental-theorem

**People:** Isaac Newton (1642–1727) and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716)  
**Era:** Scientific Revolution  
**Key contribution:** Tangency and accumulation stop being separate collections of geometric tricks. Newton frames differentiation and antidifferentiation as direct and inverse problems of changing quantities; Leibniz supplies an operational notation in which differentiation and summation can be calculated, composed, and taught. The fundamental theorem makes the two operations one calculus.  
**Paper:** `corpus/raw/library-papers/newton-method-of-fluxions-1736.pdf` — Newton, *The Method of Fluxions and Infinite Series*, written primarily in 1671; English translation by John Colson, 1736.  
**Paper:** `corpus/raw/library-papers/leibniz-early-mathematical-manuscripts-1920.pdf` — J. M. Child, trans., *The Early Mathematical Manuscripts of Leibniz* (1920), translating Leibniz’s manuscripts of 1673–1677 from C. I. Gerhardt’s Latin editions.

## The Problem That Made This Necessary

By the middle of the seventeenth century, Europe had many pieces of calculus without a calculus.

- [[../00-Ancient-Greeks/Archimedes - Method of Exhaustion|Archimedes]] could prove particular areas and volumes by exhaustion.
- [[../04-Early-Calculus/Cavalieri - Indivisibles 1635|Cavalieri]] compared continuous figures through parallel slices.
- [[../04-Early-Calculus/Fermat - Adequality and Tangents 1637|Fermat]] found tangents and extrema through small perturbations.
- [[Barrow - Geometrical Lectures 1670|Barrow]] made the reciprocity between tangent and area problems geometrically visible.

But these remained largely **problem-specific methods**. A tangent construction did not automatically produce a general language for rates. An area construction did not automatically become an operator that could be inverted. Every new curve threatened to require a new trick.

The missing idea was not merely another formula. Mathematics needed a reusable system that could answer two converse questions:

1. Given a changing quantity, what is its instantaneous rate of change?
2. Given the rate of change, what accumulated quantity produced it?

Newton and Leibniz independently made those questions two directions of one method. That unification is the real birth of calculus.

## Key Ideas

### 1. Newton turns quantities into motion

Newton imagined mathematical quantities as **fluents**: quantities generated by continuous flow. Their instantaneous velocities were **fluxions**.

If `x` and `y` flow, Newton writes their fluxions as `ẋ` and `ẏ`. A curve is no longer only a static geometric object; it is traced by changing coordinates. The tangent slope is the ratio of their velocities:

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} \quad \longleftrightarrow \quad \frac{\dot y}{\dot x}.
$$

This viewpoint makes change systematic. In *The Method of Fluxions*, Newton reduces the subject to two central problems:

> **I.** “The Length of the Space described being continually given; to find the Velocity of the Motion at any Time proposed.”  
> **II.** “The Velocity of the Motion being continually given; to find the Length of the Space described at any Time proposed.”

The first is differentiation. The second is integration or antidifferentiation. Newton explicitly calls the second the converse of the first.

This is the conceptual hinge: rate and accumulation are not neighboring topics. They are inverse problems.

### 2. The area function has the original curve as its derivative

Let a continuous function `f` define an accumulated area

$$
A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt.
$$

Increase `x` by a small amount `h`. The new strip has area

$$
A(x+h)-A(x).
$$

Because `f` is continuous, that thin strip is squeezed between rectangles whose heights approach `f(x)`. Therefore

$$
\frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h}\to f(x),
$$

so

$$
A'(x)=f(x).
$$

This is one modern form of the **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus**: accumulating a continuous rate creates a function whose derivative is that rate.

The converse computational form follows. If `F'(x)=f(x)`, then

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a).
$$

An area that once required infinitely many limiting pieces can be computed from two endpoint values. The theorem does not merely connect two definitions; it converts accumulation into algebra.

### 3. Leibniz makes the calculus operational

Newton’s motion language was powerful, but Leibniz built the notation that made the subject portable.

In his October 1675 manuscripts, Leibniz introduced the elongated `S`—`∫`, for *summa*—to represent summation. The same manuscript sequence develops `d` as the inverse operation and explores rules that become the product rule and integration by parts. By November 1675 he was writing expressions recognizably ancestral to modern differential and integral notation.

The symbols encode the structure:

- `dx` — a differential change in `x`;
- `dy/dx` — the ratio of differential changes;
- `∫ y dx` — a sum of contributions `y` across changes in `x`.

Leibniz’s notation is not decoration. It is a compact machine for preserving dependencies. The differential tells us **with respect to what** a quantity changes; the integral sign tells us **what is being accumulated** and across which variable.

That design outlived the metaphysical uncertainty around infinitesimals because it supports valid algebraic transformations and scales to partial derivatives, multiple integrals, differential equations, and variational calculus.

### 4. Independence mattered; dissemination mattered too

Newton developed his calculus first, with decisive work in 1665–1666 and a treatise completed largely in 1671. He circulated manuscripts but published late.

Leibniz developed his calculus independently in the 1670s, published the differential calculus in 1684 and the integral calculus in 1686, and cultivated a school that included the Bernoullis. The later priority dispute obscured the more important lineage fact:

- Newton supplied a deep kinematic conception of quantities and their rates.
- Leibniz supplied the notation and operational grammar through which continental analysis expanded.

Modern calculus inherits both, but its everyday written language is overwhelmingly Leibnizian.

### 5. The calculus was powerful before it was rigorous

Neither Newton nor Leibniz possessed the modern definitions needed to state the theorem exactly as above. Newton appealed to moments, ultimate ratios, and flowing quantities. Leibniz used infinitesimals—quantities smaller than ordinary finite quantities but manipulated through consistent rules.

The methods worked extraordinarily well, yet foundational questions remained:

- What exactly is an infinitesimal?
- When does a limiting process exist?
- When may an infinite sum be differentiated or integrated term by term?
- What assumptions on a function make the fundamental theorem true?

Those are not objections from outside the lineage. They are the problems the new calculus itself created. [[../04-Analysis/Euler Infinite Series 1748|Euler]] expanded its formal power; Fourier pushed representation beyond smooth formulas; [[../04-Analysis/Cauchy Limits 1821|Cauchy]], Riemann, and [[../04-Analysis/Weierstrass Epsilon-Delta 1861|Weierstrass]] rebuilt its foundations through limits and quantified convergence.

## Historical Context

The seventeenth century made change impossible to avoid. Planetary motion, falling bodies, optics, navigation, ballistics, and the geometry of new curves all demanded mathematics that could reason about variable quantities.

Analytic geometry had already converted curves into equations. That success produced a new pressure: once a curve is an algebraic relation, can its tangent, maximum, area, velocity, and accumulated effect also be calculated algebraically?

Barrow’s geometric lectures brought tangent-area reciprocity close to a theorem, but Newton’s fluxional method and Leibniz’s differential notation changed the scale of the answer. They did not solve one curve. They created a symbolic technology for whole classes of changing systems.

The publication history shaped adoption. Newton’s *Method of Fluxions* was written decades before its 1736 English publication. Leibniz’s shorter 1684 and 1686 papers appeared in print and gave other mathematicians symbols they could immediately extend. A discovery becomes a lineage only when others can work inside it.

## Lineage

### What came before

```text
Archimedes
  → areas and volumes by exhaustion, one figure at a time
Cavalieri
  → accumulation becomes systematic slicing
Fermat
  → tangents and extrema through local perturbation
Barrow
  → tangent and area problems become geometrical converses
Newton & Leibniz
  → direct and inverse problems become one symbolic calculus
```

The decisive inherited problem was fragmentation. Tangents, velocities, areas, and sums existed, but not yet as instances of one pair of inverse operations.

### What this enabled

```text
Newton & Leibniz
  → differentiation and integration as general operators
  → Fundamental Theorem: local rate ↔ global accumulation
  → Euler: functions, differential equations, and infinite series
  → Fourier: functions represented by modes
  → Cauchy / Riemann / Weierstrass: rigorous limits and integration
  → Euler–Lagrange: optimize whole functions and trajectories
  → Cauchy descent: use derivatives as an iterative algorithm
  → backpropagation: propagate local derivatives through compositions
```

The next problem was therefore unavoidable: the calculus could compute astonishingly well, but its infinite and infinitesimal operations lacked a stable foundation. That is why Euler’s expansion and the nineteenth-century rigor crisis logically follow.

## Why It Matters for AI

### 1. Backpropagation is calculus organized over a graph

A neural network is a composition of functions. Backpropagation repeatedly applies the chain rule to compute how the final loss changes with every parameter:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_n}
\frac{\partial h_n}{\partial h_{n-1}}
\cdots
\frac{\partial h_1}{\partial w}.
$$

Newton and Leibniz did not invent backpropagation, but they made the derivative a reusable local operator. Reverse-mode automatic differentiation is the computational descendant of that move: calculate local rates, compose them, and recover global sensitivity.

### 2. Training turns derivatives into action

Gradient-based learning asks how a scalar loss changes under a small parameter change, then moves in a direction predicted to reduce it:

$$
\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\nabla L(\theta_t).
$$

That update requires the differential side of calculus. [[../11-Optimization-Theory/Cauchy - Steepest Descent 1847|Cauchy]] later turns this local sensitivity into an iterative optimization algorithm; stochastic approximation and modern optimizers adapt it to noisy data and high-dimensional models.

### 3. Expected loss is accumulation

Machine learning does not only differentiate. It also integrates:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p}[\ell(f_\theta(x),y)]
=
\int \ell(f_\theta(x),y)\,dp(x,y).
$$

Expected risk, marginal likelihood, Bayesian prediction, diffusion models, and continuous probability all accumulate contributions over distributions. Modern training repeatedly moves between the two sides of the old theorem: define a global accumulated objective, then differentiate it to obtain local parameter updates.

### 4. Notation became executable structure

Leibniz understood that good notation can carry reasoning. Modern tensor libraries push that principle further: an expression written as a composition of differentiable operations becomes a computation graph, and the system mechanically derives its gradients.

The line from `d` and `∫` to automatic differentiation is not that notation magically became software. It is that calculus first made transformations of change sufficiently systematic to be mechanized.
